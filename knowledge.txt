ADHD brains struggle with task initiation, not just attention — the barrier is getting started, not staying focused once started.
Breaking a task into the very first physical action (e.g. "open the document", "write one sentence") dramatically lowers initiation resistance.
The Pomodoro technique works particularly well for ADHD because it creates artificial urgency and a defined endpoint — the brain only has to commit for 25 minutes.
Externalizing time with visible countdowns helps ADHD users who experience time blindness (inability to feel how much time is passing).
Body doubling — working alongside someone, even an AI — can significantly improve focus and reduce procrastination for ADHD individuals.
ADHD paralysis is not laziness. It is a neurological barrier caused by dopamine dysregulation making initiation feel impossible.
Small wins release dopamine. Breaking big tasks into tiny checkable steps creates a dopamine reward loop that sustains momentum.
The 2-minute rule: if a task takes less than 2 minutes, do it immediately. This builds momentum and clears mental overhead.
Positive reinforcement is far more effective than guilt or shame for ADHD. Celebrate what was done, not what wasn't.
ADHD brains are driven by interest, challenge, urgency, and novelty — framing a task as a challenge or game can help initiation.
Task switching costs are high for ADHD. Protecting a single focus session from interruptions is critical.
The 5-second rule (count 5-4-3-2-1 and physically move) can interrupt avoidance loops and force action.
Cold water, exercise, and high-protein foods (tyrosine) can increase baseline dopamine, improving focus naturally.
Hyperfocus is real — ADHD users can lock in deeply on tasks they find genuinely interesting. Use this by finding the interesting angle.
Rest is not optional. ADHD brains deplete faster during focused work. Breaks are not laziness — they are maintenance.
